(Sex)-specific trajectories of resilience after adolescent social stress

Stress-related psychiatric conditions are a leading cause for disability world-wide and why individuals differ in their vulnerability to chronic stress remains poorly understood. Specifically, it is unclear when behavioural trajectories that are associated with later stress susceptibility emerge and how they relate to spatially organized molecular adaptations in the brain. Here, we combine longitudinal automated homecage phenotyping, conventional analyses, and spatially resolved proteomics in male and female mice exposed to adolescent social instability stress. We find that spontaneous behavior during the early phase of stress exposure carries prospective information about later resilience and susceptibility. Subsequent behavioural phenotypes reveal dynamic adaptations across repeated stress exposure rather than a simple, stable separation between resilient and susceptible animals. Spatial proteomic profiling further shows that stress exposure produces highly organized molecular remodeling across hippocampal regions and cellular compartments, with resilient and susceptible animals exhibiting both shared and divergent responses in metabolic, RNA-regulatory, and neuronal signaling programs. Together, our findings indicate that resilience and susceptibility emerge as distinct paths that can be detected early in behaviour and are accompanied by spatially-structured molecular adaptations in the hippocampus.
Main
The nature of psychiatric disorders, including depression, is complex and multifaceted on not just the origins of development, but also in the symptomatic appearance. The causes for the development, or the heightened susceptibility to develop are thought to be of mainly genetic and environmental factors. Stress-related psychiatric disorders such as depression, anxiety, and PTSD are among the most prevalent mental health conditions world-wide, and go hand in hand with a high contribution to societal and economic burden1,2. Chronic stress poses as a main risk factor for those psychiatric disorders, yet individuals exposed to comparable stressors can show markedly different long-term outcomes. This heterogeneity is particulary relevant during adolescence, a period of continued neural maturation in which adverse experiences can have lasting consequences for emotional and cognitive function5–7 (also Romeo & McEwen, 2006). Nevertheless, stress exposure does not uniformly lead to persistent dysfunction. Some individuals develop pronounced behavioral and physiological alterations, while others maintain or recover adaptive function8. Understanding the biological basis of this variation between stress susceptibility and resilience therefore requires resolving not only the consequences of stress, but also how different outcomes emerge over time. Sex is an additional
source of heterogeneity, as stress-related psychiatric disorders differ between women and men in prevalence and clinical presentation, while preclinical studies have historically focused disproportionately on males (ref)9,10,11,12.
Experimental studies of stress resilience and susceptibility commonly rely on behavioural measurements obtained after stress exposure, often using one or a small number of conventional tests (ref). Such approaches have been instrumental in identifying individual variation in stress responses, but provide only brief observations of behaviour under experimentally imposed conditions. Moreover, classifications made after stress can not establish whether the behavioural features associated with later outcome were already present early during exposure or instead developed progressively as animals adapted to repeated stress. Multidimensional phenotyping across behavioural, physiological, and somatic measures can provide a more comprehensive description of stress outcome, but still leaves this temporal dimension largely unresolved.
Continuous homecage monitoring gives a complementary means of capturing this process. By repeatedly measuring spontaneous behaviour with minimal experimental intervention, homecage systems can quantify features such as locomotor activity, spatial organization, social proximity, and rest-activity structures across prolonged periods (refs). Such longitudinal measurements may therefore reveal behavioural trajectories that are not apparent from conventional endpoint assays. Critically, they also provide the opportunity to ask whether spontaneous behaviour early during stress exposure contains prospective information about an individual’s later phenotype. This distinction is important if resilience and susceptibility represent different trajectories of adaptation rather than fixed states that become apparent only after stress has ended.
The molecular consequences of chronic stress are similarly heterogenous. Stress-induced changes differ between brain regions, cell populations, and cellular compartments, yet many molecular studies of stress outcomes have relied on bulk tissue measurements that average across this anatomical organization (refs). The hippocampus is particularly relevant in this context because of its involvement in stress regulation, memory and behavioural adaptation, and because subregions and cellular compartments differ substantially in organization and function (refs). Spatially resolved molecular profiling therefore offers the possibility of distinguishing broadly shared reponses to stress from molecular alterations associated with resilient or susceptible outcomes, while preserving the anatomical context in which these changes occur.
Here, we combined multidimensional behavioral and physiological phenotyping with longitudinal automated homecage monitoring and spatially resolved proteomics in male and female mice exposed to adolescent social instability stress (SIS). We asked whether behavioural features associated with subsequent resilience and susceptibility can be detected from the earliest stages of stress exposure, how behavioural organization changes over repeated exposure, and how later stress outcome is associated with molecular remodeling across hippocampal regions and cellular compartments. This framework allowed us to examine resilience and susceptibility as temporally developing behavioural and molecular trajectories rather than solely as phenotypes defined at the end of stress exposure.
Adolescent social instability produces heterogenous behavioral and physiological outcomes
To address the question whether SIS evokes individual-specific responses on the behavioral and physiological realm, we performed a battery of behavioral tests following the two-week social instability paradigm (Fig. 1a). Social instability induced a heterogenous response in both males and females. Behavioral tests were automatically analysed using SLEAPanalyzer and showed high correlation with manually scored ground truth (Supp. Fig. x).
Early spontaneous home-cage activity predirct later composite stress outcome
Adolescent social instability is driving responses and later outcomes that differ markedly between individuals. The question we aim to address is whether later divergence is foreshadowed by behavior early during stress exposure. To answer this, we separated the measurement timeline into a single early observation window and a set of later outcome measures. RFID tracking began at the start of the SIS paradigm. The early phase consisted of the first 12h active phase between 18:30 and 06:30. We used 10 min bins (Fig. 1b, Supp. Fig. 5a). The later outcome was summarized as a compositve z-score (CombZ), the mean of those six components, each z-scored against same-sex control animals and with delta corticosterone, adrenal weight, and spleen weight sign-inverted so that all six point in the same direction. Higher CombZ indicates a more resilient-like outcome, lower CombZ corresponds to a greater later stress burden. Stress-exposed animals were classified as susceptible when CombZ fell more than one control standard deviation below the same-sex control mean, and resilient otherwise.
With this design, mean movement over the early wondow was negatively associated with later CombZ (Spearman ρ = −0.39, 95% CI [−0.55, −0.21], q = 6.9 × 10⁻⁵; n = 111 animals, 58 female and 53 male, comprising 24 control, 49 resilient and 38 susceptible; Fig. 1c). Therefore, animals that were more active during the first undisturbed night after the cage change showed a lower later CombZ, meaning that they show a less resilient-like outcome and greater later stress burden. Short-timescale variability (RMSSD) was also associated in the same direction (ρ = −0.23, q = 0.026). Lag-one autocorrelation of binned activity entropy did not reach FDR support (ρ = −0.18, q = 0.067; Supp. Fig. 9a).
Next, we asked if this association carries prospective information about individual animals. For this, we used raw movement as the predictor. Held-out performance was estimated by leave-one-animal- out cross-validation. This movement-mean model explained approximately 17% of the variation in later CombZ in held-out animals (R2 = 0.173), against -0.018 for an intercept-only baseline. A repeated grouped five-fold scheme with the animal as the grouping unit (k = 5, 100 repeats) gave a closely matching estimate (mean R2 = 0.156; 2.5th-97.5th percentile range across repeats 0.116-0.179), and a permutation test that repeated the entire fitting and cross-validation procedure under permuted outcomes placed the observed value beyond every one of 1000 draws (p = 1/1001; Fig. 1d). Adding the two remaining features, or sex, did not improve on mean movement alone (Supp. Fig. 5b).
Early spontaneous behavior therefore carries prospective information about where an animal’s later composite outcome will fall.
Spatially-resolved hippocampal proteomics recover reproducible anatomical molecular organisatiuon
We asked whether spatial proteomic profiles preserved the expected anatomical organization of the hippocampus. Therefore, we characterized the hippocampal proteome across the three distinct compartments. Laser-capture microdissection followed by data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry generated 323 spatial proteomic acquisitions from nine animals, comprising 180 neuropil, 71 neuronal soma, and 72 from microglia-enriched ROI measurements. Here, we identified a median of 4668, 5307, and 4850 protein groups per acquisition, respectively (Fig. 2a,b). The neuropil was sampled at region-by-layer resolution, neuronal soma and microglia were sampled at region level, with soma being sampled exclusively from the sp (CA1-CA3) and the sg (DG) regions. After filtering, the three analysis matrices comprised 5054, 5538, and 5229 protein groups, with 4242 protein groups detected in all three compartments.
Compartment, not the stress group, dominated the global structure of the dataset. In a joint principal-component analysis of the 4242 shared protein groups across all 323 acquisitions, the first two components captured 45.1% and 9.8% of the variance, and anatomical compartment accounted for essentially all the variance along PC1 (η² = 0.96) whereas experimental group accounted for effectively none (η² = 8.5 × 10⁻⁵; Fig. 2c).
Importantly, within the three control animals, the proteome defines a coherent anatomical fingerprint. Using eleven pre-specified control-only anatomical contrasts and rank-based, phenotype-blind slection, 19 protein rows separated the 18 spatial units into anatomically coherent blocks (Fig. 2d), and ten prespecified compartment markers were most abundant in their expected compartment (Fig. 2e).
Bilateral similarity varies more by measurement compartment than by anatomical scale. Comparing the per-protein anatomical contrast estimate obtained independently from each hemisphere of the same three control animals, left-right Pearson correlation was highest for neuronal-soma regional contrasts (median 0.80, range 0.72-0.92), intermediate and widest in the neuropil (median 0.74, range 0.38-0.85), and lowest for the microglia-enriched ROI (median 0.56, range 0.50-0.76; Fig. 2f, Supp. Fig. 1a). Importantly, anatomical scale was not the discriminating factor: dentage-gyrus layer contrasts reproduced as well as the better regional contrasts (r = 0.81), and CA1 stratum lacunosum-moleculare (r = 0.74) sat at the inventory median. The two lowest values in the inventory were both CA1 laminar: stratum oriens (r = 0.38) and the stratum radiatum (r = 0.49). They were followed by the four microglia-enriched regional contrasts (r = 0.50 – 0.76).
Consistent with hemispheres being repeated tissue rather than independent samples, averagine the two hemispheres raised the interclass correlation scores from a median of 0.33 to 0.50 for compartment scores and from 0.32 to 0.48 for reference-marker scores (Supp. Fig. 1b), which is why all downstream inference is performed on bilaterally aggregated, animal-level values.
Finally, the neuropil and neuronal-soma spatial assignments recapitulated and confirmed independently reported hippocampal anatomy. We tested nine control-only anatomical contrasts (five neuropil, four neuronal soma; three control animals) against seven hippocampal subregion and synaptic signatures reported by an independent study (Kaulich et al., 2025). All ten a-priori expected contrast-signature pairings were recovered in the expected direction and all ten were supported under the stored signature-family correction (normalized enrichment score 1.95-3.69 for positive direction pairings; Fig. 2g, Supp. Fig. 2b). No external anatomical signature was available for the microglia-enriched compartment, which is therefore not externally anchored.
Together, these results demonstrate that we resolved hippocampal molecular organization which is reproducible between the two hemispheres of the same animal. At the anatomical scale each compartment supports region x layer in the neuropil and ergion level in the neuronal-soma and microglia-enriched compartments.
Later resilient and susceptible outcomes are associated with sparse protein-level and coordinated, spatially-resolved molecular program differences We next asked if and how later resilient and susceptible outcomes are respresented in thise spatially-resolved proteome. At the level of individual proteins, they are represented sparsely. Across all 18 spatial untis, 37 proteins reached FDR support for the susceptible vs resilient contrast, and 12 of the 18 units contained no proteins reaching FDR support (Fig. 3a). Of the 37 reaching FDR support, 28 were in a single neuropil unit, CA2 stratum-moleculare.
However, following quality-control, we can report only six, which is due to the CA2-SLM showing the highest pre-imputation missingness of the ten neuropil units (6.2-24.8% across acquisitions) and that missingness was itself unequal between groups. Applying the prespecified robustness criteria, six of the 28 CA2-SLM proteins qualified; the 9 FDR-supported proteins outside CA2-SLM did not have this artefact and enter unchanged, giving 15 rubsutness-qualified proteins in total (Fig. 3a, Supp. Fig. 3).
Coordinated differences at the level of molecular programs are detectable. Interestingly, this also applied to units where no individual protein reaches FDR support. To summarise them, we mapped the canonical gene-set enrichment results onto a curated atlas of seven ontology-defined program families. These were RNA processing / splicing / RNP organization, translation / ribosome biogeneis, chromatin organization / epigenetic regulation, mithochondrial respiration / oxidative phosphorylation, synaptic signalling / vesicle-mediated transport, neuron projection development, and autophagy / endolysosomal trafficking (253 constituent GO biological-process terms; Fig. 3b, Supp. Fig. 6a,b). FDR-supported constituent terms occur in 132 of the 378 theme x unit x contrast cells, and as observed those supported cells are distributed unevenly across spatial contexts and across the three contrasts. The atlas shows the cell colour as the median normalized enrichment score of a family’s constituent terms and contains 26.7% of FDR-supported GO occurences and 14.0% of unique supported GO identifiers.
Three respresentative programs, one per measurement compartment, are shown (Fig. 3c-f). Each was chosen from the terms already FDR-supported for the susceptible-versus-resilient contrast in that compartment. Throughout, gene-set FDR is conditional on the ranked per-gene contrast statistic and is a statement about gene ranks rather than about the three animals per group.  A synaptic-signalling program in CA3 stratum radiatum neuropil (GO:0099536) was lower in susceptible animals relative to both resilient animals and controls (NES -1.72, FDR 4.1 × 10-8; NES -1.72, FDR 7.4 × 10-6), with no support differences between resilient and control individuals (FDR 0.19). An mRNA-processing program in CA2 neuronal soma (GO:0006397) showed the same asymmetry in the opposite direction (NES 2.13, FDR 1.9 x 10-7 and NES 1.62, FDR 0.028; resilient-versus-control FDR 0.78). Both are therefore susceptibility-associated. By contrast, a reduced oxidative-phosphorylation program in the CA1 microglia-enriched ROI (GO:0006119) was supported in all three contrasts in the same direction (FDR 5.8 × 10⁻⁵, 1.5 × 10⁻⁸ and 3.1 × 10⁻³) and is better described as a graded, stress-associated direction than as an outcome-specific one.
Therefore, we observed a general and spatial-specific shift of resilient animals’ proteome in most of the described groups in the proteome atlas towards an increase of enrichment, while the susceptibility phenotype is mostly characterized by heterogenous shifts towards decreased, especially in mitochondrial respiration, and increased enrichment scores or no change, compared to controls. Due to the shift of pathways in the opposite direction of resilient animals, and this is painting a picture of not just a susceptibility phenotype, but also an active shift of resilient individuals, which show that resilience is not just the absence of a behavioral phenotype, but more an adaptive process on the molecular side.
4 Discussion
Resilience and susceptibility as outcomes of heterogenous adaptation to the same stress experience
Information about later outcomes is already present in spontaneous behaviour early during exposure
Eventual phenotypes are accompanied by spatially heterogenous patterns of molecular remodeling
It is intriguing to speculate, that behavioral phenotyping early on during the development might help with assessing risk factors of later in life maladaption to stressors during development. This might also help understanding, why some individuals develop psychopathology and why other are seemingly untouched by adverse experiences during childhood. Furthermore, this paper highlights the individuality of the stress response and it impact on different behavioral outcomes in both males and females.
Proteomics: differential findings, resilience and susceptible animals both change during stress, resilience associated with relatively broad but specific upregulation of pathway families in either broad hippocampal areas or specific layers. 2 Methods
2.1 Animals
Male and female C57BL/6J mice between the ages of 20-58 days (bred in-house at the animal facility of the Max Delbrück Center for Molecular Medicine) were used for all experiments. Until the start of the social instability stress protocol, the animals were housed in standard individually ventilated macrolon type II cages. Animals were kept with a 12-hour dark/light cycle with food and water ad libitum. The animals were housed in a temperature-controlled environment (22 ±2°C) with 40-60% humidity. All experiments were performed during the light phase. During the social instability procedure, cage cleaning and bedding replacement was conducted weekly following a standardized scheme for all experimental batches. All experimental procedures complied with institutional and European animal welfare guidelines and were approved by the Landesamt für Gesundheit und Soziales, Berlin, Germany. All possible efforts were made to reduce the number of animals used according to the 3R principle.
2.2 Social Instability Stress
Social instability stress commenced 3-4 days following the implantation of the RFID chips between the ages of 24 days – 27 days. Experimental animals of each behavioral cohort were born within a timeframe of 4 days, ensuring similar ages and developmental stages.
The social instability stress was performed by shuffling animal group compositions following the social golfer paradigm twice per week, resulting in a total of 4 cage changes during the 14 day paradigm. After the 14-day SIS period, the animals remained within their last social group and behavioral testing started immediately afterwards.
2.3 Homecage phenotyping
Raw RFID position data were processed in R using the BehavioralDynamics analysis workflow. PhenoSys AnimalPos files were processed separately for each experimental batch (B1-B6) and cage-change session (CC1-CC4). Raw timestamps were parsed while retaining sub-second precision, and recorded coordinates were mapped onto the eight RFID position of the 2 x 4 homecage grid. Animals specified in the predefined exclusion list were removed before behavioral feature extraction. Active and inactive phases were determined using the clock time, with the active phase spanning from 18:30-06:30 and the inactive phase from 06:30-18:30. Consecutive phase blocks were indexed from timestamps, and ncomplete recording periods at the beginning and the end of individual cage changes were removed.
Because RFID position records predominantly represent changes in detected position, continuous occupancy intervals were reconstructed by carrying forward the most recently observed position until the next recorded position change. For animals for which the first recorded event occurred after the beginning of the analysed recording window, the initial position was recovered from the last valid raw RFID observation. Occupancy intervals were therefore divided at phase and aggregation boundaries and summarized at multiple temporal resolutions (10 s, 1 min, 5 min, 10 min, 30 min).
Movement was quantified as the number of RFID position transitions within each time bin, with movement distance additionally calculated as the summed Manhattan distance across the RFID grid. Spatial behavioral organization was quantified using the Shannon entropy
Further processing used timestamp-aware binning of events for movement (i.e. positional changes and detection by separate coils). Derved measures are movement, entropy, social proximity. Secondary analyses include curve descriptors such as rmssd and acf1 in order to describe the complex behavioral structure. Data were filtered to exclude incomplete datasets and specific phases, with additional adjustments for grid-in-cage conditions.
Assessment of sleep phases Sleep was defined as a period of inactivity lasting more than 60 seconds, building on the established method of identifying sleep through sustained immobility. This approach aligns with prior research16, demonstrating inactive periods exceeding 40 seconds strongly correlating with sleep EEG patterns in mice.
2.4 Behavioral Tests
Behavioral testing was conducted between 8:00 AM and 4:00 PM to ensure consistency across experimental conditions. Automated analysis of the Open Field Test (OFT), Novel Object Recognition (NOR), Elevated Plus Maze (EPM), and Social Preference Test (SocP) was performed using a customized version of the DLCAnalyzer framework. This modified tool builds upon the original functionality by integrating additional code to enhance its capabilities, including support for SLEAP tracking data and advanced behavioral metrics such as interaction angles, proximity measures, and latency calculations. The modifications allow for more refined and detailed analyses of behavioral data across these paradigms. The custom code is publicly available on GitHub at SLEAPanalyzer, enabling reproducibility and further development by the research community. All automatized pipelines were benchmarked with manual scored behavioral analysis using BORIS (supp. Fig. x).
Sucrose Preference Test The sucrose preference test commenced the following day after the 14-day SIS paradigm. Animals were habituated to access of water through a grid placed on top of the cage for two hours during the light phase for three consecutive days prior to testing for sucrose preference. For sucrose preference testing, the animals were placed into a standard Macrolon type II cage with bedding and permitted to acclimate for 60 minutes on three consecutive days. Following the habituation, two bottles were placed on top of the cage, one with 2% sucrose solution and one with water. The order of sucrose and water was randomized between trials for each animal to avoid place preference bias. After each two-hour session, the bottles were removed and the animals were returned to their home cages. The bottles were then weighted and a ratio was formed between the sum of sucrose and water intake over the three sessions as: .
Social Preference Test The social preference test started one day following the end of the sucrose preference test. The animals were transferred to the experimental room one hour before the test. The test was performed in a custom-made arena (measurements here). During the first phase of the test, the animal was introduced into the arena and allowed to explore the arena and both empty cups for 10 minutes. Following, in the second phase, one stranger mouse was introduced into one cup and the test subject was allowed to explore to interact with it for 10 minutes. Following, another stranger animal was placed into the remaining cup and the test subject was able to freely chose interaction partners and explore the arena. Following this, all animals were removed from the arena and returned to their homecages.
Open field test The open field test was performed two days following the social preference test. The open field test arena was made up with a 50 x 50 cm arena, by TSE. The values of speed, center time and time in the periphery as well as the distance travelled in the center and the periphery were automatically recorded.
Elevated plus maze
The elevated plus maze contained four arms, two open arms and two closed arms. The animals were placed into the center area of the maze facing the open arm and able to freely explore all four arms for a total duration of 5 minutes. Time on the open arms was seen as a less anxious behavior.
2.4.2 Automatization of behavioral test analysis
To facilitate unbiased and analysis of behavior, we used SLEAP to created pose estimations for each individual in the Social Preference and Novelty Preference test (SocP), the Novel Object Recognition test (NOR), and the Open Field test (OFT) as well as the elevated plus maze (EPM). Additionally, the different arenas were tracked to extract key points and facilitate accurate measurements of distances and speed, as well as allowing for the automatic definition of regions of interest, such as objects or social zones. Following, arena and animal coordinates were combined into one single data frame and analyzed using DLCAnalyzer14. DLCAnalyzer allows for out-of-the-box analyses of the EPM and OFT, however does not support analyses of behavioral tests involving objects or interaction zones. Therefore, we built analyses for the NOR and SocP that also include the integration of object, zones, and head orientation, dubbed SLEAPanalyzer. The code for running automated analyses of the OFT, EPM, SocP, and NOR is available on GitHub15.
2.5 Molecular measurements
2.5.1 Blood sampling For blood corticosterone measurements, the animals were placed on a stainless-steel grid and the tail was carefully restrained by the experimenter, allowing the animal to move freely. Using a scalpel, a small horizontal incision is then made a few centimeters from the base of the tail from which up to 50 µl of blood were collected using microvettes (. After collection, bleeding is stopped by gently pressing a cotton pad on the incision. The animals are then gently returned to their holding cages. Blood is drawn only once from one group of animals
2.5.1 Corticosterone level measurement
Basal and reactivity plasma samples were thawed on ice and diluted 1:10 in corticosterone diluent to a final volume of 80 µl. Plasma corticosterone concentrations were quantified using a Corticosterone (Human, Rat, Mouse) ELISA kit (IBL International GmbH, Hamburg, Germany) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Samples were assayed in triplicate and included in subsequent analyses only when the intra-assay coefficient of variation across technical replicates was <10%.
2.5.2 Transcriptomics of dHip
For transcriptomic profiling of the dorsal hippocampus, brains were sectioned into 200 µm coronal slices. Tissue punches encompassing the dorsal hippocampus, including the CA1, CA2, CA3, and DG, were collected using a sample corer. Up to eight punches were pooled per animal to generate a single bulk RNA sample respresenting the dorsal hippocampus without layer or region resolution. Three biological replicates were analysed per experimental condition.
2.5.3 Spatial Proteomics
2.5.3.1 Preprocessing of hippocampal slices
Briefly, 10 μm brain slices were mounted on Leica PPS membrane slides and stained for NeuN, Iba-1, and DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired using either a Keyence BZ-X microscope or an Olympus spinning disk at x40 or x20 magnification and imported into QuPath (version 0.5.0) for anatomical annotation. Regions of interest (ROIs) were assigned to dorsal hippocampal subregions CA1, CA2, CA3 and the dentage gyrus (DG). Within each hippocampal subregion, ROIs were defined to distinguish microglial somata, neuronal soma-enriched layers and neuropil compartments. Neuropil ROIs were selected from the stratum oriens (SO), stratum radiatum (SR), and stratum lacunosum-moleculare (SLM) of CA1-CA3, and from the polymorphic (PO) and molecular (MO) layers of the DG, while avoiding neuronal and microglial cell bodies. Neuronal soma-enriched ROIs were selected from the stratum pyramidale (SP) of CA1-CA3 and the granule cell layer (SG) of the DG. Thus, this sampling strategy enabled spatial separation of neuronal soma-enriched, microglial soma-enriched, and neuropil compartments. Individual ROIs covered an area of 750-800 um2. ROIs were subsequently exported from QuPath and converted using a previously described workflow17 for downstream processing.
2.5.3.2 Laser Microdissection and Proteomics
For each animal, 8 brain slices were processed, with 500 to 800 contours collected for each slice. Contours were cut and sorted into a 384 well plate ( cat no. 0030129547, Eppendorf twin.tec® PCR Plate 384 LoBind®, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). Collected tissue was stored at 4°C until further processing. Sample preparation for LC-MS was initiated with the denatureion of proteins by adding 2 µL of lysis buffer to each sample well, followed by incubation at 95°C for 1 hour in a thermocycler. For initial digestion, 4 ng of Lys-C (prepared dilution: 2 µL) was added to each well, and the samples were incubated at 37°C for 2 hours in a thermocycler. Subsequently, 6 ng of trypsin (prepared dilution: 1 µL) was added to each well for further digestion, and the samples were incubated at 37°C overnight in a thermocycler. After overnight incubation, the digest was stopped by spinning the plate for 40 min at 60°C using a SpeedVac. The samples were then stored at -20°C until Evotip cleaning.
Following the preparation of the samples, the samples were dissolved in 10 µL of Buffer A, vortexed, and centrifuged at 700 g for 1 minute. For tip conditioning, 20 µL of Buffer B was added to each tip in Evotip Box #2, followed by centrifugation at 700 g for 1 minute. Subsequently, 20 µL of Buffer A was added to each tip, soaked in isopropanol for 10 seconds, and centrifuged again at 700 g for 1 minute. To prevent drying, the tray was transferred to Evotip Box #1 containing Buffer A. For sample loading, 10 µL of the dissolved sample was added to the corresponding tips. The sample plate was rinsed with an additional 10 µL of Buffer A, centrifuged, and the rinse was transferred to the corresponding tips. The samples were then centrifuged at 700 g for 1 minute to allow passage through the tips. For tip cleaning, the tray was transferred back to Box #1, where 20 µL of Buffer A was added to the tips. Finally, the tray was moved to Box #2 and centrifuged at 700 g for 1 minute. Following, 100 µL Buffer A were added to the tips and the tips were centrifuged for 15 seconds at 700 g to cover the membrane and prevent drying out. The samples were then stored at 4 °C until further processing.
2.6 Tissue collection
Animals from batches 1-4 were perfused with 4% PFA, animals from batches 5 and 6 were perfused using only PBS.
2.7 Statistics
Behavior - classical testing - homecage testing
Homecage behavior was analyzed at both the initial response and longitudinal levels. by fitting linear mixed-effects models using the lmerTest package on 30 minute bins of activity. To account for repeated measures and individual variability, random effects were incorporated on the animal level. Pair-wise post-hoc comparisons between groups were performed using the using the emmeans function from the emmeans package. All data was visualized using ggplot2, cowplot, tidyr, and gridExtra packages
